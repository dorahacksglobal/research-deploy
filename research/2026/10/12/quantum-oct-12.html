From Scalable Logical Qubits to QUOPS

12 Oct 2026

DoraHacks Quantum Newsletter

Troyer, Nayak and Martinis propose a common framework for scalable logical qubits. Proctor et al. measure circuit capability and throughput across physical and logical processors. We extend their application comparison with secp256k1 resource estimates from Google, Schrottenloher and ECDSA.Fail.

0. Takeaways

A scalable logical qubit needs repeated error correction, universal fault-tolerant operations and real-time feedback, together with a credible path to larger systems. Troyer, Nayak and Martinis organize these requirements around reliability, scale, capability and performance.[1]

Proctor et al. introduce QUOPS to measure the size of circuits a quantum computer can execute and its effective throughput.[2] Their physical-qubit experiments reach 216 QUOPS on Google Willow and 1,504 on Quantinuum Helios-1, or 1,824 with leakage postselection. A Steane-code implementation reaches 40 QUOPS. The same benchmark exposes both the benefits of connectivity and the present cost of fault tolerance.

Using the paper's resource-matching method, we place secp256k1 targets at roughly 1,200–1,500 logical qubits and tens of millions of QUOPS. The latest ECDSA.Fail point-addition record gives a conditional estimate of 1,266 logical qubits and QUOPS. It remains outside the capability regions of the paper's two projected architectures.

1. Introduction

Quantum error-correction experiments now report logical memories, encoded gate sequences and processors with several logical qubits. These demonstrations test different capabilities. Preserving an encoded state through repeated correction establishes a memory result. Running an algorithm also requires logical gates, measurements and classical decisions while the encoded state remains protected. A logical-qubit count needs this context before it can support a comparison.

Our June newsletter, Better Gates, Better Codes, Better Randomness, compared sustained neutral-atom QEC with code demonstrations across hardware platforms. Our July newsletter, RL-Driven Quantum Error Correction and the ECC Resource Frontier, followed improved QEC control and lower ECC resource estimates. Those developments leave a common question: how much computation can a processor sustain, and how does that capability compare with an application?

Matthias Troyer and Chetan Nayak of Microsoft Quantum, together with John Martinis of QOLAB, address the reporting problem in Scalable logical qubits.[1] Timothy Proctor and collaborators at Sandia, Quantinuum, the University of New Mexico and NVIDIA address measurement in Benchmarking the computational power of quantum computers.[2] The first proposes a definition and assessment framework; the second introduces a benchmark and reports hardware experiments.

We read the two together, then add ECC-256 resource targets to the benchmark's architecture projections. Here ECC-256 means the secp256k1 elliptic curve. The comparison connects a familiar application with the logical width and circuit size that a fault-tolerant processor would need.

2. Scalable logical qubits

Repeated correction must support computation

Troyer, Nayak and Martinis define a scalable logical qubit through a path toward sustained, useful computation.[1] Repeated QEC must preserve the encoded information while the processor applies a universal set of fault-tolerant operations. A classical controller must decode measurement results and supply feedback fast enough to support conditional operations during execution.

The definition also requires predictable improvement. Increasing protection within a code family should lower the logical error rate, and the architecture should support replication to hundreds or thousands of logical qubits while preserving performance.

A memory experiment provides one part of the evidence. Researchers prepare an encoded state, run correction cycles and measure how long the information survives. During a program, the processor must also interact with other logical qubits and ancillary blocks. Fault-tolerant gate constructions have to control how errors propagate through those interactions. The classical control system then has to handle measurement-dependent decisions without leaving the rest of the computation waiting beyond its error budget.

Troyer, Nayak and Martinis's proposed requirements connect logical operations, syndrome extraction, decoding and feedback with improving protection and scalable replication.[1]

Four dimensions of logical performance

The authors organize progress into four coupled dimensions.[1] Each adds information that a logical-qubit count leaves unspecified.


Dimension

Evidence needed to assess a logical processor

Reliability

Logical errors under repeated correction, including how error rates fall as protection increases

Scale

The number of logical qubits that can operate together, and how quality and latency change as the system grows

Capability

Supported fault-tolerant gates, measurements and real-time conditional control

Performance

Logical-operation latency, program runtime and the resource cost of reliable execution


With a fixed physical-qubit budget, a team can encode more logical qubits at modest protection or fewer at stronger protection. A larger logical count can therefore accompany a higher error rate. The useful choice depends on the circuit: a wide, shallow workload and a narrow, deep workload may prefer different allocations.

Time introduces another trade-off. A decoder can improve its decisions by using more computation, but the controller still needs those decisions before dependent quantum operations proceed. A code with low spatial overhead can also require more steps to implement a logical gate. Reporting the cycle time alongside the full logical-operation latency makes these costs visible.

For demanding workloads, the authors discuss logical error targets around –, depending on the application and its allowed failure probability.[1]

From memory to universal control

The capability checklist covers logical state preparation and measurement, memory, Clifford operations, non-Clifford operations and measurement-conditioned control.[1] Researchers need to establish which of these work under repeated correction and how their errors accumulate when combined.

Non-Clifford operations often require magic states. A processor prepares an ancillary resource state and consumes it to implement a gate outside the Clifford set. State preparation can fail or require repeated attempts, and injection may need a correction conditioned on a measurement. A useful benchmark should include the cost of this sequence along with the quality of the final gate.

Postselection and error mitigation can improve particular tasks. Discarding runs with detected errors raises the quality of the retained data, while mitigation uses additional samples and a noise model to recover information from imperfect circuits. Their contribution to performance depends on how many executions the user needs. A logical result with excellent conditional fidelity should therefore also report its retention probability and time cost.

Once a processor supports the required instructions, a user needs to know which circuit shapes it can execute at acceptable quality and how much time those executions take. QUOPS supplies a common test for a specified family of circuits.

3. QUOPS: circuit size and effective throughput

A common circuit family

Gate fidelity measures local operations. Quantum Volume tests whole circuits by finding the largest passing square circuit, with equal width and depth. QUOPS allows width and size to vary independently and adds a measure of effective throughput.[2] Researchers can use it to compare the behavior of different execution stacks, including compilation, routing and logical encoding.

QUOPS stands for quantum universal operation performance system. Its circuits alternate random single-qubit Pauli rotations with CNOT layers on randomly paired qubits. The rotation axis and angle vary, and the CNOT pairs can span the circuit. Each platform compiles this shared instruction set into its own operations.

That compilation carries physical consequences. A device with local connectivity must route a CNOT between distant qubits. A Clifford+ processor must approximate an arbitrary-angle rotation using a sequence of discrete gates. Both routing errors and synthesis error reduce the quality of the implemented circuit. The benchmark includes those costs in the result.

A common circuit family incurs different compilation costs on physical-qubit and fault-tolerant architectures. Proctor et al.[2]

The authors choose rotation-heavy circuits partly because they resemble product-formula Hamiltonian simulation. For algorithms dominated by Toffoli gates, we use the paper's non-Clifford resource conversion below.

All circuit shapes that pass this process-quality threshold form a capability region. A device can support wide circuits of limited size and much larger circuits at narrower widths.

Mirror circuits make quality measurable

A generic large random circuit can be expensive to simulate on a classical computer, which makes direct verification difficult. Proctor et al. use mirror circuit fidelity estimation, or MCFE.[2] The experiment embeds a circuit in a randomized mirror construction with a known ideal output. Reference circuits allow the analysis to estimate the quality of the original circuit while accounting for the additional operations used in the measurement procedure.

The mirror return probability contains errors from the whole constructed sequence. MCFE uses reference measurements and its compilation rules to estimate the original circuit's quality, avoiding a classical calculation of its full output distribution. The score tests use a confidence level of at least 95%.

Willow and Boston reach similar maximum sizes, while Willow has much higher effective throughput at its reported operating point. The trapped-ion systems sustain larger circuits but execute them at lower rates. Their all-to-all connectivity reduces the routing burden of random CNOT pairs. In the locally connected superconducting devices, routing becomes more expensive as the circuit widens.

The rates include experimental choices as well as hardware timing. Willow's throughput experiment uses large batches of repeated shots to amortize circuit-switching costs. IBM's rate uses a batch-time approximation, and the Quantinuum timing includes operational overheads within the measurement window.[2]

Widths of 6 or 16 identify the shapes that maximize the score. The processors can run wider circuits at other sizes. Their full capability regions contain this information, which the single-number summary omits.

The measured cost of fault tolerance

On Helios-1, the researchers also execute the benchmark on up to eight Steane logical qubits.[2] They implement Clifford gates transversally and supply gates through magic-state injection. The final version protects the circuit against a fault at any single location, including errors that can propagate between composed gate operations.

This implementation reaches 40 QUOPS at width 4 and 4.9 QUOPS/s. The score includes the errors and time costs of logical control and rotation synthesis. The original size-40 circuits contain 20 arbitrary-angle rotations, which the chosen synthesis approximates using an average of 10 gates before mirroring.

Successive implementations show how the benchmark can track software improvements on fixed hardware. Better magic states and synthesis randomization raise the score from 24 to 40. Revised QEC placement and improved parallel execution then increase the rate from 1.3 to 4.9 QUOPS/s while strengthening composable fault tolerance.[2]

The encoded implementation has a lower score than the physical-qubit implementation. Its small code and synthesis overhead define the measured operating point. To examine how larger protected systems could improve on it, the authors project the capability regions of two fault-tolerant architecture families.

Converting application resources to QUOPS

The original figure places two application targets beside these regions: RSA-2048 factoring and energy estimation for a 76-orbital FeMoco model.[2–4] Both algorithms consume large numbers of Toffoli gates. QUOPS instead counts rotations and CNOTs, so the authors compare their non-Clifford resource requirements.

They first use a resource cost of four gates per Toffoli, appropriate to the compute/uncompute constructions assumed in the estimate. They then determine the -gate quality needed for the algorithm to meet the chosen polarization threshold and optimize the balance between rotation-synthesis error and -gate execution error. The model neglects Clifford and idle errors.

Google and Schrottenloher provide two ECC working points each

Babbush et al., with authors from Google Quantum AI, the Ethereum Foundation and Stanford, report secp256k1 constructions below 1,200 logical qubits and 90 million Toffolis, or below 1,450 qubits and 70 million Toffolis.[5] They disclose resource budgets and a zero-knowledge verification procedure while keeping the point-addition circuits private. We plot the rounded budget endpoints from the corrected v2 of the paper.

Schrottenloher publishes an open point-addition construction and a complete-algorithm resource model.[6] Adding the 16-bit window register gives widths of 1,208 and 1,462 for the low-width and low-Toffoli variants. Their converted sizes are about 27.3 million and 21.9 million QUOPS.

For both sources, the low-width construction uses more Toffolis, while the low-Toffoli construction uses more qubits.

Mapping the latest ECDSA.Fail kernels to QUOPS

The public ECDSA.Fail challenge optimizes a reversible secp256k1 point-addition kernel and ranks submissions by peak live qubits multiplied by average executed Toffolis.[7,8] At our check on 28 September 2026, 13:06 Hong Kong time, the best product record uses 1,250 qubits and 889,047 Toffolis per point addition. The corresponding code commit is 03d9ecd.[8]

Lower arithmetic cost still requires sufficient width

The ECC points lie to the left of the original RSA and FeMoco targets. Under the shared mapping, these selected secp256k1 constructions need a smaller equivalent circuit size. Their logical widths remain around 1,200–1,500. None lies within the projected capability regions shown here.

A processor must support the required size and width together. For the latest ECC target, that means a capability region covering QUOPS at a width of 1,266 logical qubits.

Hardware and QEC improvements can expand the shaded regions; better arithmetic can lower an application's resource demand. Google's budgets, Schrottenloher's open constructions and the ECDSA.Fail records show how algorithm development changes the target. With the QUOPS mapping, we can compare those changes with improvements in hardware capability.

5. Conclusion

Troyer, Nayak and Martinis propose reporting logical qubits together with their reliability, supported operations, scaling behavior and performance.[1] Proctor et al. make part of this comparison measurable through a shared circuit family.[2] Their experiments show why circuit size and throughput need separate reporting, and why a small logical implementation can incur substantial overhead even when its operations satisfy fault-tolerance requirements.

The next benchmark milestone is a fault-tolerant implementation that exceeds the best physical implementation under the same standard. Current resource models place secp256k1 at roughly a thousand logical qubits and tens of millions of equivalent QUOPS. Recent point-addition optimizations lower the estimated size further. Measuring capability across the required widths will show how closely logical processors approach these application targets.
