Better Logical Qubits, the Cost of Magic States, and Leaner Circuits

28 Oct 2026

DoraHacks Quantum Newsletter

Quantinuum reports lower logical errors in color-code memories and Clifford circuits. A study of magic-state cultivation revises the cost of supplying non-Clifford resources. An LLM-guided search finds cheaper reversible circuits.

0. Takeaways

Quantinuum's Helios processor reaches a logical memory error rate of per correction round using a distance-7 color code. A separate distance-5 experiment reaches errors per logical two-qubit Clifford. Both results show improving protection as code distance increases.[1]

Magic state cultivation can require more quantum resources than earlier estimates assumed. In the surface-code protocol studied by Mehta et al., preserving the output state requires a larger code distance and more correction rounds during the escape stage to recover the target quality.[2]

Gu et al. combine LLM proposals with an independent evaluator to redesign reversible quantum circuits. Seven benchmark tasks reduce both Toffoli and qubit counts, with the comparison baseline specified for each task. A new reversible divsteps construction also supplies a different space-versus-gate-count choice for elliptic-curve point addition.[3]

1. Introduction

A quantum error-correction experiment can preserve a logical state more reliably while a useful algorithm remains too expensive to execute. The algorithm needs enough logical qubits, a long sequence of reliable gates, and the resources that supply those gates. Progress in any one of these quantities changes the requirements on the others.

Our July newsletter, RL-Driven Quantum Error Correction and the ECC Resource Frontier, followed improved control of Google's Willow processor and smaller logical circuits for elliptic-curve cryptanalysis. This issue follows progress in logical memory and Clifford operations, examines the cost of supplying non-Clifford resources, then considers how circuit design can reduce an algorithm's resource demand.

Quantinuum reports color-code experiments on Helios, testing whether increasing code distance improves both logical memory and encoded Clifford operations.[1] Harvard and MIT researchers examine the cost of magic-state cultivation when its output must remain available for computation.[2] The researchers from Harvard, QuEra and Anthropic study how LLM-guided search can reduce the logical resources needed by reversible circuits.[3] Their search changes how those circuits perform arithmetic and retain intermediate information.

Quantinuum measures logical error rates for memory qubits and Clifford operations on hardware. Mehta et al. estimate the cost of the non-Clifford resource states needed to complete a universal gate set. Gu et al. then ask how better circuit designs can reduce the logical gates and qubits an algorithm requires. The studies connect experimental reliability, the supply cost of non-Clifford operations and the resources demanded by an algorithm.

2. Quantinuum pushes color codes further below threshold

Larger color codes reduce logical memory errors

Quantum error correction encodes a logical qubit across several physical qubits. Auxiliary qubits measure stabilizers, producing a syndrome that gives information about errors without directly reading the protected logical state. A decoder uses that record to infer a correction. Increasing the code distance gives the code more protection, while also adding physical operations that can fail. Below threshold, the additional protection wins and the logical error rate falls.

Color codes support transversal Clifford operations: a logical operation acts across corresponding physical qubits without spreading one fault arbitrarily within a code block. Helios's all-to-all connectivity supports these interactions and the syndrome-extraction schedule.

Leakage removal makes repeated correction effective

The physical qubits are encoded in two levels of a ion. An error can move population into nearby levels outside this computational space. Such leakage can persist across rounds, interfere with later gates and spread errors to other qubits. A syndrome decoder cannot compensate indefinitely for a physical carrier that remains outside the intended qubit states.

The experiment addresses leakage at two levels. A hardware repump sequence transfers leaked population back into the computational space. Three repump cycles suppress the leakage population by .[1] At this operating point the repump overlaps cooling and transport, so it adds no circuit time. Returning population to the qubit space still leaves an error for ordinary QEC to handle.

The circuit also rotates which physical ions carry data. Fresh ancillas take over the data role, while previous data carriers move into positions that undergo measurement and reset. Three alternating syndrome-extraction schedules refresh every data position. Some SWAP operations can be implemented through software relabeling on Helios; the subsequent reset is what clears persistent leakage.

The energy-level diagram shows the repump transitions in . Circuit-level role exchange gives data carriers access to reset, and hardware repumping further reduces the logical error on top of this schedule.[1]

The results distinguish these contributions. With circuit-level leakage reduction already in place, adding hardware repump improves the logical error rate by approximately fourfold at distance 3 and eightfold at distance 5.[1] The two mechanisms address leakage before it can accumulate over repeated correction rounds.

Encoded Clifford operations improve too

A logical memory tests preservation. Computation also requires operations between encoded states. Quantinuum therefore runs randomized benchmarking on two logical qubits, each encoded in a distance-3 or distance-5 color-code block.[1] Random two-qubit Clifford sequences are followed by their inverse and a final measurement. The sequences contain 69, 139 or 209 Cliffords, with one syndrome-extraction round inserted after each group of seven gates. Two distance-7 blocks exceed the available capacity, so the logical-gate experiment stops at distance 5.[1]

Classical decoding remains part of the implementation problem. Convolutional and transformer decoders trained on simulated Helios data also show improving memory performance with code distance. At distance 7 their average GPU processing times are approximately 0.52 ms and 0.35 ms per syndrome round on an NVIDIA H100.[1] All decoding in this experiment was performed offline. These timing measurements support future integration, while communication, controller overhead and measurement-conditioned operations still need to work in a live feedback loop.

The memory and Clifford experiments establish two components of a logical processor. Universal computation also needs non-Clifford operations, commonly supplied by preparing and consuming magic states. Their quality and preparation cost determine what it takes to extend this hardware progress to a universal gate set.

3. What a usable magic state costs

Supplying non-Clifford operations

Clifford operations alone do not provide universal quantum computation. One way to implement a non-Clifford gate is to prepare a special ancillary state, then consume it through a measurement-and-feedback circuit. An algorithm with many such operations needs a continuing supply of sufficiently accurate magic states.

Cultivation starts with a noisy resource state in a small code. Checks and postselection reject attempts with detected errors; the retained state then enters an escape stage that grows its protection.[2] Rejected attempts consume resources too. For identical complete attempts, expected cost per accepted state would be the attempt's spacetime volume divided by its acceptance probability. A multistage protocol must account for where rejected attempts stop, as well as the resources used by successful ones.

Google's experiment on Willow demonstrates cultivation and a method called kickback tomography for bounding the resulting state's fidelity.[4] The authors report a fidelity lower bound of approximately 0.9999(1), with about a 40-fold error reduction relative to injection alone. They also demonstrate transferring a cultivated state from a distance-3 color code into a distance-5 surface code.

The reported 8% retention belongs to a tomography benchmark that filters detection events across the complete preparation-and-measurement circuit.[4] It is a useful experimental statistic, but the retained shots have supplied information through measurements that need not be available when delivering an unmeasured state. Mehta et al. examine that information difference in cultivation resource estimates.[2]

The acceptance decision cannot use future readout

At the end of a benchmark, the output code block can be measured to determine the logical result and obtain further syndrome information. A resource estimate may use that record to judge which attempts should have been accepted. The simulation convention examined by Mehta et al. supplies terminal information equivalent to an ideal QEC round.[2]

During computation, the factory must decide whether to accept a state while preserving it for a later gate. That decision can use the stabilizer measurements already performed, but it cannot use a destructive output readout that has yet to occur. The authors call these two information conditions a closed and an open temporal boundary.

An open boundary still permits stabilizer measurements and ongoing error correction. It restricts which information can enter the acceptance decision while the output remains available.[2]

The missing terminal record changes how confidently a decoder can distinguish a safe attempt from a risky one. In a benchmark, some failures can be rejected using that extra record. Preserving the output removes this option. The factory may need stronger inference, stricter postselection or more quantum resources to achieve the same conditional error rate.

Better inference reduces the postselection penalty

Mehta et al. introduce Caliper, a method that estimates the logical failure risk from the syndrome available at decision time.[2] It searches over possible hidden terminal syndromes and weighs their likelihood and logical consequences. It then accepts attempts that meet the selected confidence threshold.

More escape space and time can recover quality

The compared cultivation protocols initially use different escape resources. The surface-code protocol grows to distance 7 and uses three rounds before the acceptance decision. The corresponding color-code protocol uses a distance-15 grafted code and nine rounds, supplying a much larger syndrome record.[2] This motivates varying the escape code distance and duration together.

The heatmaps hold escape acceptance at 68%. The separate cost-optimized working point discussed below uses 50% acceptance, so it should not be read directly from this fixed-acceptance slice.[2]

These costs attach a physical resource budget to each non-Clifford operation. Reducing the number of costly operations in an algorithm can lower the total demand. Gu et al. address that part of the problem by redesigning reversible circuits.[3]

4. LLMs redesign reversible circuits

Reversibility changes the optimization problem

Many quantum algorithms repeatedly evaluate classical functions on quantum registers. Arithmetic, numerical functions and table lookups must work coherently when the inputs are in superposition. A program cannot inspect a quantum input and choose an ordinary classical branch. It implements the alternatives through controlled operations, and temporary information must eventually be removed without disturbing the output.

A small example makes the cost visible. Suppose inputs must remain unchanged while an output bit becomes . One construction computes into a clean ancilla , copies it into with a CNOT, then reverses the first computation to reset . It uses two Toffolis and four qubits. A direct Toffoli controlled by and targeting performs the same task using one Toffoli and three qubits. For the first construction, clearing matters because leaving it correlated with the data would generally disturb a superposition if it were discarded.

This teaching example illustrates why the algorithm and its cleanup must be designed together. Gu et al. apply that idea to much larger circuits, where changing the arithmetic representation or recovering intermediate information can save far more than local gate cancellation.[3]

An independent evaluator guides the search

The framework asks an LLM to write high-level Python programs that generate circuits. An independent evaluator tests the resulting operation stream, checks its functional behavior and counts its resources.[3] The search first proposes distinct algorithm families, then improves candidates within those families and shares successful ideas between them.

Efficient evaluation depends on the chosen circuit class. For a computational-basis input, the allowed operations can be tracked using a bit string and its phase, with measurement-based cleanup handled explicitly. This avoids storing a general state vector with exponentially many amplitudes. Correct action on every basis input, with the required phases and cleanup, would establish coherent action by linearity. Large input domains are tested by sampling, however, and some tasks allow a specified failure probability. The evaluator's guarantee therefore depends on the task specification and the tests used.

Divsteps moves the point-addition trade-off

Modular inversion finds an integer such that , for coprime to . This is the modular inverse of . For example, , so . Elliptic-curve point addition uses this operation to evaluate a slope in a finite field, making inversion an important part of its resource cost.

Divsteps replaces expensive division with conditional additions, subtractions and halving. Making the integer computation reversible is difficult because the successive branch choices must be undone. Gu et al. recover readily decodable decisions from arithmetic state and store only the harder cases.[3] Their construction uses a bounded history stack and a bounded stopping schedule. The selected parameters carry explicit probabilities of stack overflow and incomplete convergence, which belong in the accuracy budget.

The paper compares four constructions with the same affine-coordinate point-addition interface, including reversible cleanup. Its new divsteps point uses 935 logical qubits and Toffolis. Schrottenloher's construction uses 1,446 qubits and Toffolis in that comparison.[3,5] Divsteps saves about 35% of the qubits while using roughly twice the Toffolis. Its TQ product is therefore higher at these selected points.

The paper's common-interface comparison includes cleanup and excludes application-specific storage or lookup for the classical addend. The 935-qubit, four-million-Toffoli result is for point addition, not a bare inversion or the complete Shor algorithm.[3]

The same table includes Luo's packed Euclidean construction at 838 qubits and Toffolis, and a specialized Euclidean construction at 843 qubits and Toffolis.[3,6] The latter exchanges five additional qubits for a substantial gate reduction. These alternatives show how algorithm design changes the cost even within a similar space budget.

The public ECDSA.Fail challenge provides a separate, changing comparison. At our check on 27 October 2026, its best product score was 915,170,186, using 1,234 qubits and a rounded average of 741,629 executed Toffolis.[7] The submission reports approximations and a selected test packet; its failure guarantees require separate assessment before comparison at a common accuracy target. We therefore keep it outside the paper's four-point plot. None of these logical counts includes error correction, factories or the full algorithm schedule.

5. Conclusion

Quantinuum's experiments show improving color-code performance in both memory and Clifford operations.[1] Extending these results to sustained universal computation requires reliable non-Clifford operations and integrated feedback.

Mehta et al. examine the resource states that supply those non-Clifford operations.[2] Their cost depends on the information available when the factory makes its acceptance decision. Some targets remain close to earlier estimates; others require larger escape codes and longer correction sequences.

Gu et al. reduce the workload that the processor and its resource-state factories must support through changes to reversible algorithms, data routing and cleanup.[3] The divsteps example keeps the choice of objective visible: a smaller logical circuit width can come with more Toffolis and a higher product cost. Together with measured logical reliability and magic-state production costs, these circuit counts provide inputs to an architecture-specific resource estimate.
